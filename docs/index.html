Lawn Mowing Experiment

By Russell Samora, Arjun Kakkar, Florina Sutanto, and Sushant Karki

Mowing a lawn. Vacuuming a room. Shoveling snow. You’ve probably done these tasks hundreds of times without thinking much about it. Some part of your brain works out a route that’s usually good enough to get the job done. People are actually pretty good at this, and better than you’d guess for a problem that is hard for computers. We wanted to see it for ourselves, so we built a lawn and asked people to mow it.

An Optimal path

A few weeks ago, 20,305 people mowed the same lawn. Here is what their paths look like.

Many came close to the optimal path. 55% of people came within five moves of the best possible path, and 18% did it perfectly.

Many different approaches, yet similar, pretty good outcomes. Only 49 squares needed to be covered, but people found 9,640 different ways to do it. Despite all that variety, the median person still hit 91% efficiency (within 5 moves).

For this small lawn, there were 12 different perfect solutions and people found them all.

But what were you actually solving?

Finding the best path through the squares is a classic computer science problem. No, don’t leave! We promise this won’t get too wonky.

Path planning problems

Solving the lawn mowing problem is formally called Coverage Path Planning, and is related to one of the most famous path planning problems in computer science: The Traveling Salesman Problem.

The premise is simple: a salesman must visit a set of cities exactly once and return to their starting point, all while taking the shortest possible route. We see this all the time in the real world. A pizza delivery driver planning their stops or a trick-or-treater maximizing their candy haul is solving a version of this problem.

We wanted to create this mowing experiment after we read a study that found humans come impressively close to computer-calculated optimal solutions to this problem with fewer stops, and even do pretty good — just 11% less efficient than optimal — for 70+ stops. But as the stops increase, humans’ ability decreases. Would results be similar for mowing a lawn?

A slight detour, if we may. It is worth noting that there are two main ways to tackle a problem like this:

  • algorithms which calculate the optimal path and
  • heuristics which are simple strategies to complete the path

Why do we need heuristics? If we look at the brute force calculations for a salesperson visiting just 10 cities, we’d surprisingly need to test 3.6 million possible routes. That number explodes exponentially as more cities are added.

15 cities and we’re at 1.3 trillion possible routes. 😱

20 cities balloons to 2.4 quintillion possible routes. 🤯

When problems are small, the approach doesn’t matter much, almost everything works. But as complexity grows, optimal becomes impossible and heuristics become necessary. This is true for both humans and computers. We both fall back on good-enough strategies when perfect is impractical.

With the maths out of the way we can get back to the good stuff.

A guided tour

Let’s replay a mowing session, director’s-cut style, with some commentary along the way. For this walkthrough, we’ll use a perfectly average run from player yo7m5rr3nl, who called themselves Bones. It took them 54 moves–five more than optimal. We recorded the timestamp of each move, so every hesitation or direction change left a data footprint.

We’ll rewind to the start and walk through it. Before Bones even makes a move, they pause for 2.9 seconds. They were perfectly average here as well; the median first-move pause for all players was three seconds. Upon seeing the lawn revealed, they naturally pause to survey the lawn and, perhaps, make a plan.

Off goes Bones! There is nothing to decide in the opening stretch until the fifth square. Continue right, or go down?

This is where players’ paths first diverge. Bones barely hesitates (0.7s) and decides to go down, like 21% of other players. This is a critical moment that we’ll come back to shortly.

Bones plods along the outside edge until they reach the bottom. Their first real pause (2.4s) comes when they realize they’ve hit a dead end and will have to retrace their steps.

After realizing their mistake, Bones turns around and re-mows their first square to finish this section. To get to the other open section they must backtrack through two more squares. Then it is pretty much smooth sailing as they work around the edges, then fill in the remaining squares in the middle.

What differentiated the great players from everyone else comes down to a single critical mistake. We can divide the lawn into two main sections: left and right. Like most players, Bones chose to complete the left section first, forcing an inevitable backtrack because of this one-way corridor.

If we look at where every player ended up we can see this play out. Players who were three or more moves from optimal finished all over the lawn.

While those who were nearly or perfectly optimal chose to mow the right side first so they could end their run on the left side, having anticipated the one-way trap.

One of those was player X. We tracked them down–discovered their name was X–and asked them, why were they so awesome at this game?

Six lawns

That was one lawn. We gave players five more, each bigger and more cluttered than the last, up to a 14x14 lawn with nearly 200 squares to cover.

show all lawns?

Here is how everyone did across all of them.

people who completed all levels optimality scores

We expected a decline. The Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP) research suggests that people do worse as the problem grows. They didn’t. Optimality held around 90% from 25 squares to 196, almost 8x larger.

Our best guess is the lawn itself. Where TSP scatters nodes randomly, a lawn has structure, which lets players fall back on two strategies: decomposition and compression.

Players were able to decompose the lawn into smaller, more manageable sections and solve them incrementally—clear this empty space first, then that corner, and finally the space between the rocks.

As they moved between rounds, they compressed what they learned into a mental model of the game, allowing them to identify patterns in more complex lawns and adapt their gameplay.

Also, we may not have found the inflection point. Our largest lawn was 14x14. We don’t know whether the trend holds or breaks as it gets larger.

When you think

Bones took X seconds to mow the lawn and finished in the middle of the pack. TK how typical that was.

The obvious theory here is that players who thought longer would mow better. But total mow time had almost no relationship to optimality.

chart

But where people paused is a different story.

pause heatmap of bonus2, top vs worst players

The top players spent time thinking early as they traveled down the first column, then dedicated most of it at this one juncture: fork toward the cluttered middle section, or clear the open space to the right first.

highlight pause spots for top players

The worst players sped through the beginning and barely paused to think until a corner or edge forced them to, implying reacting, not planning.

highlight pause spots for worst players

The top players were able to anticipate where they should end in order to minimize backtracking: this little pocket at the top. They didn’t need to pause once they reached the home stretch. The worst players only realized they were trapped once they arrived and were forced to stop, backtrack, and work out where to go from there.

highlight the ending pocket

two ghost mowers side by side on the same clock, top solver vs average reader

The best players moved at about the same pace as everyone else, but they revisited far fewer squares. They spent their time at the forks and cruised through the rest.

So it’s not about thinking more or thinking faster. It’s about when you think. That’s what a good heuristic does: it spends your attention on the decisions that matter and nothing on the ones that don’t.
